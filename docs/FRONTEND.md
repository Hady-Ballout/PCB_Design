| `pcbPlace.js` | **Netlist-aware placement**: `placeComponents(parts, { expandFactor })` → `{ placements, board }`. Deterministic greedy insertion — seed the highest-pad-count part at the origin, then repeatedly take the part most strongly connected to what's already placed (connectivity graph ignores nets touching > 4 pads, i.e. power/ground rails) and scan a 1.27 mm grid spiralling out from its placed connections, trying rotations 0/90/180/270. Each candidate is scored by the Manhattan distance from its pads to the nearest placed pad on the same net and rejected if its courtyard, inflated by `PLACEMENT_GAP`/2, hits another's (`PLACEMENT_GAP`/`BOARD_MARGIN` re-export `RULES.placementGap`/`RULES.boardMargin` from `pcbDesignRules.js`). The scan stops one ring after the first ring that yielded a valid spot. `expandFactor > 1` spreads the finished placement apart by `sqrt(expandFactor)` (routing-retry room); the result is then shifted into `BOARD_MARGIN` and the board snapped up to whole mm. No RNG, no clock — same circuit in, byte-identical placement out. Also exports `placementBounds`/`placementPads` (rotation applied) and `isUnconnectedTerminal`/`unwiredPadNet`. **`placementPads` emits the WHOLE footprint**, not just the wired nodes: one entry per circuit node in node order, then every record pad no `padOrder` entry claimed, named `NC_<ref>_<padNumber>` so `isUnconnectedTerminal` recognises it. A DIP-8 op-amp uses five nodes but the chip has eight legs — a board drilled for five of them cannot take the part, yet every gate would still call it fabricable. Those appended pads flow on as real copper with no net: router obstacles (`pcbRoute`'s `#nc:` keys), DRC clearance participants, Gerber copper flash + mask opening + drill hit, and a `.kicad_pcb` pad with no net node |
| `pcbDesignRules.js` | The one home for every fabrication number: `RULES` (`gridPitch` 0.635 = 2.54/4, `traceWidth` 0.8, `traceWidthLadder` [0.8, 0.5, 0.4, 0.25] + `minTraceWidth` 0.25 (the router's neck-down rungs; 0.25 mm is ~2× JLCPCB's 0.127 mm 2-layer floor and carries ~1 A of 1 oz copper at a 10 °C rise), `clearance` 0.3, `viaDiameter`/`viaDrill` 1.2/0.6, `edgeClearance` 0.5, `boardMargin` 4, `placementGap` 2.0, `maskExpansion`, `silkWidth`, `boardThickness`), the annular-ring minimums (`PAD_ANNULAR_RING` 0.13, `VIA_ANNULAR_RING` 0.2 — quoted **per side**, checked as `inscribed diameter ≥ drill + 2 × ring`; 0.13 is JLCPCB's stated 2-layer minimum, which is what the fab-proven KiCad 6.0.11 TO-92 pad needs at 0.15 mm/side) and the two pad-copper models, which exist because keep-out and containment need opposite kinds of error. `padCopperRadius` is the **clearance over-approximation**: the circumscribing circle (`hypot(w,h)/2` for rect pads, `max(w,h)/2` otherwise), which never under-reports copper and is therefore safe as the DRC clearance check's cheap reject and as a bounding radius for the router's candidate-cell scans — but it is *not* a containment test (a 3×3 rect pad's circle reaches 0.62 mm past the pad's own edge). `padCopperShape`/`padCopperDistance`/`copperCorePoints` are the **shape-exact** model: a pad is a convex core inflated by a radius (rect → rectangle, oval → stadium, circle → point, anything unrecognised → its bounding rectangle, which is the conservative direction for containment), and `padCopperDistance(pad, x, y)` returns the signed distance to the true copper boundary (≤ 0 inside). Every "is this point on the pad?" question — the router's terminal-cell pick, `checkConnectivity`'s touch test — uses the exact one. `padCoreDistance(shape, x, y)` is the same measurement taken to the *un-inflated* core, which is the quantity the router's grid-edge sag bound applies to. `size` is already board-space (`pcbPlace` swaps w/h at 90/270 and `pcbLayout` deliberately drops `angle`), so `angle` is the pad's residual tilt within that frame and is 0 for everything the vendored library ships. `pcbPlace.js`, `pcbRoute.js`, `pcbDrc.js` and `pcbLayout.js` all read from here |
| `pcbRoute.js` | **Clearance-aware two-layer A\* maze router**: `routeBoard({ components, board }, rules)` → `{ traces, vias, failedNets }`. Nets are split into two-terminal jobs by a Manhattan MST over their pads (`spanningEdges`); jobs run shortest-first with ground-like rails (`GROUND_NET_RE`, exported for the pour) last, and each job's goal is *every* cell already electrically on the net, so later joins land as T-junctions. A* on a `gridPitch` grid × 2 layers: step 1, +0.5 against the layer's preferred direction (top = horizontal, bottom = vertical), +0.5 per bend, via 8; heuristic is an exact two-pass L1 distance transform to the goal cells; the binary heap breaks ties on (f, h, y, x, layer). Obstacle masks are rebuilt per job from the copper of every *other* net into `Int32Array` owner maps (one for traces, one for the larger via disk). **Correctness argument**: all routed copper is grid-aligned, and the minimum distance between two grid-aligned segments is always attained at cell centres, so centre testing is exact for trace↔trace; vias and escape-cell disks are centred *on* grid nodes, where the nearest point of a grid edge is itself a grid node, so they need no correction either. Pads are the one off-grid obstacle, and they are stamped from their **exact shape**: block a cell when `padCoreDistance(pad, cell) < hypot(r + clearance + traceHalf, pitch/2)`, i.e. the sag bound `sqrt(D² − (pitch/2)²)` applied to the pad's *core* (rectangle / stadium spine / point) rather than to a circumscribing disc. The module header proves that bound for an arbitrary obstacle set, not just a circle. Keeping `r` inside the hypot matters: `hypot(r + C, p/2) < r + hypot(C, p/2)`, and on a TO-92 oval that difference plus the 0.225 mm the bounding disc invents *is* the escape corridor out of the middle pin. A pad's terminal cell is always usable by its own net, so every foreign pad is also stamped as a `traceWidth + clearance` disk around *its* terminal cell. That terminal cell is chosen by `padCopperDistance` against the pad's **exact** outline, not the keep-out circle — the sort prefers the cell with the most room, i.e. the outermost candidate, so an over-approximating containment test would put trace endpoints beside rect pads rather than on them. **Neck-down ladder**: each terminal cell records `limit`, the widest trace half-width that can sit on it *and* leave it (the sag bound inverted against every foreign pad); a job is laid at `traceWidth` and steps down `RULES.traceWidthLadder` only when a rung's escape or search fails. The obstacle mask is rebuilt for the width actually being laid — that smaller inflation is the entire mechanism — while every other net's laid copper contributes its own real width, so a neck is never bought at a neighbour's expense. Width is per **job**, so a two-terminal connection is one uniform width and each emitted trace carries its own `width`. This is what makes TO-92/BJT boards routable: the middle pin of a 1.27 mm-pitch TO-92 escapes at 0.25–0.4 mm where 0.8 mm does not fit at all. A pad with no room even at the bottom rung is reported `no_escape` instead of routed into a short. What a rung may touch is rebuilt **per rung**, not just the mask, because the mask is only half the story — the search seeds its sources directly and force-allows pad terminal cells past the mask: at each rung the job's own two endpoints must clear `escape.limit ≥ half`, the force-allowed terminal set holds only the pads of the job's two islands that clear it too, and a source cell is seeded only when the mask leaves it free or it is one of those admitted terminals (no seedable source ⇒ fall to the next rung). The invariant is narrow but exact — every copper cell a rung may occupy has been proved safe *at that rung's width* — and it is what stops a later job inheriting room only a narrower earlier job ever had (full-width copper landing on a pinched pad's terminal cell, or a job starting from a necked run's cell that now sits inside a foreign pad's larger keep-out). The exception never applies to the board edge, and never to the net's other copper cells (a full-width job joining a necked run must clear the mask at *its* width). On failure the two nets owning the most blocked cells around the closed set are ripped up and re-queued (2 rip-ups per board). Fully deterministic |
| `pcbPour.js` | **Bottom-copper ground pour**: `buildGroundPour(layout, rules)` → `{ net, layer: 'bottom', connect: 'direct', polygons }` or `null`. Runs after routing, fills what routing left over, and never moves a trace. The ground net is the `GROUND_NET_RE` match (imported from `pcbRoute.js`, not re-written) carrying the most pads — any *other* ground-like net stays foreign copper, since merging `GND` and `0` would invent a connection the circuit never asked for. The board is sampled on `gridPitch / 2` cells and a cell is kept when its centre clears every foreign copper item by `clearance +` **half a cell's diagonal** and the board edge by `edgeClearance +` the same. That margin is the whole argument: it turns a statement about sample points into a statement about filled copper, so every square micron of a kept cell clears by at least the rule. Each item contributes its **own** half-width (a 0.25 mm necked trace does not reserve 0.8 mm) and pads are measured with the shape-exact `padCopperDistance`. Kept cells are then flood-filled (4-connected) from the cells that physically land on ground pad/via copper, and anything unreachable is **dropped whole** — floating copper is an antenna and a DFM warning. The result is emitted as disjoint axis-aligned **rectangles** (maximal horizontal runs, merged vertically), not traced outlines, because both emitters union and neither has an even-odd rule: an outline of a mask full of pad-shaped holes would flood copper straight back over the keep-outs the mask just carved. Rectangles are the hole-free representation of the same copper, and being convex they are also what lets `pcbDrc` measure the pour exactly. **Direct connect** — ground pads are swallowed, no thermal spokes; the fabrication README warns that hand-soldering into the plane needs more heat. ~80% bottom-layer coverage on a typical board |
| `pcbDrc.js` | **Independent** design-rule + connectivity check — shares nothing with the router's grid bookkeeping, so it can act as the pipeline's oracle. `runDrc(layout, rules)` → `{ ok, violations }`: geometric distance between every same-layer different-net copper pair through a uniform bucket index, plus copper-to-board-edge, a `trace_width` floor at `RULES.minTraceWidth` (the neck-down ladder needs an independent backstop, or a neck could walk below what a fab can etch with every other rule still reporting clean), and annular rings (measured on the pad's **inscribed** width — a ring is thinnest across the short axis, so reading the circumscribing diameter of an oval pad was a false PASS). Clearance is a **two-tier check** — `padCopperRadius`'s circumscribing circle runs first as a cheap reject (it contains the real copper, so "circle gap ≥ clearance" proves the real gap is too), and only a pair the circle fails is re-measured against the exact outlines; only that exact measurement may raise a violation, so an over-approximating circle can neither invent a clearance error for a rect pad nor hide one. Pads and vias are copper on *both* layers; pads inside one footprint are not checked against each other (their pitch is the vendored library's business — TO-92_Inline really is 1.27 mm). The ground pour is a fourth copper kind: one convex polygon per `pcbPour` rectangle, `r = 0`, on the pour's layer — a filled convex polygon's nearest point to anything outside it lies on its **boundary**, so edge-to-edge measurement *is* the copper gap with no hole to hide a short in. The pour's own construction already guarantees the clearance; measuring it here is the independent second opinion that says so (a test feeds `runDrc` a hand-placed polygon straddling a foreign trace, to prove the checker would catch a mask bug rather than agree with it by construction). Pour polygons deliberately stay **out** of the connectivity union-find — the flood fill only keeps cells already sitting on ground copper, so letting them join could only ever paper over a net routing had left split. `checkConnectivity(layout)` → `{ ok, incompleteNets }`: union-find over each net's pads/traces/vias, joined wherever their **exact** copper physically touches (no cheap tier — an over-approximation here is a false PASS, welding a trace to a pad it physically misses), so every routed net must come out as one island |
| `pcbLayout.js` | `buildPcbLayout(circuit, { expandFactor })` — the orchestrator over five independent stages: `pcbFootprints` → `pcbPlace` → `pcbRoute` → `pcbPour` → `pcbDrc`. Places with `pcbPlace`, builds the layout object (components with placed pads, `rotation`, courtyard extents), routes with `pcbRoute`, pours the leftover bottom copper with `pcbPour` (onto `layout.pour`, `null` when the circuit has no ground net), then measures the whole thing — pour included — with `pcbDrc`. The pour goes in *before* the DRC deliberately, so the checker sees the finished board rather than the board minus its largest piece of copper. If any net fails to route it re-places and re-routes on a roomier board (expansion ladder 1 → 1.15 → 1.35 → 1.6, mirroring `routeWithExpansion` in `schematicLayout.js`) and keeps the best attempt. "Best" is `isBetterAttempt` (exported, unit-tested on its own): a **DRC-first** lexicographic ranking of `(drc.violations, routing.failedNets, connectivity.incompleteNets)` — a short is worse than an unrouted net, which is worse than a broken island — replacing the old failedNets-first comparator that let an attempt with a short outrank a clean-but-incomplete one. The result always carries its own verdict: `board.outline`, `routing: { complete, failedNets }`, `drc: { ok, violations }`, `connectivity: { ok, incompleteNets }` — a board is never silently shipped with a short or a broken net. Exports `BOARD_THICKNESS`/`TRACE_WIDTH`/`VIA_DIAMETER`/`PAD_DIAMETER` for the 3D viewer |

### How the layout engine draws a human-readable schematic

`layoutCircuitDiagram(circuit)` turns the topological circuit model (components +
net connectivity, no coordinates) into a fully placed and routed diagram. The AI
never supplies positions; all geometry is deterministic. The pipeline aims to
read like an engineer drew it rather than a machine scattered parts:

1. **Rank into signal-flow columns.** `rankComponents` BFS-walks the net graph
   from the sources; a component's rank (distance in hops) becomes its column,
   giving left-to-right signal flow.
2. **Tidy placement first.** `buildDraft` places each rank's parts in that rank's
   vertical lane using `TIDY_GRID` — a single clean column per rank (ranks with
   several parts fan into a tight aligned block). This is the preferred, most
   hand-drawn-looking layout.
   - **Pin alignment.** Before placing a part, `alignmentRow` looks for an
     already-placed pin on a shared net and lines the part up with the row that
     pin escapes on, so series passives, op-amp inputs, and BJT bases connect in
     one straight run instead of a staircase. Alignment stays within the part's
     own rank band (long chains wrap into serpentine bands and must not collapse
     onto one endless row). Multi-pin side pins (op-amp inputs, BJT C/E) sit a
     grid multiple from the component center so aligned wires meet them without
     an off-grid jog at the pin.
   - **Upright grounded passives.** A grounded 2-pin part that flips vertical
     also swaps its body to portrait so the vertical zigzag glyph fits.
   - **Labels hug their part.** `makeComponentLabels` prefers spots just past
     the body box / pin-escape reach (26px above/below, 40px beside horizontal
     parts, 10px beside vertical ones); the outward ring search is a last
     resort, since text floating away from its part reads as machine layout.
3. **Route + validate.** `routeWithExpansion` runs the grid A\* router and
   `validateDiagramLayout`. The tidy pass gets a small expansion budget
   (`TIDY_EXPANSION_BUDGET`) so it bails quickly on topologies it can't route
   cleanly (e.g. an op-amp whose input parts rank to its right and must wrap
   around).
4. **Spread fallback.** If tidy can't route, retry once with `SPREAD_GRID` — the
   original wider fan-out that interleaves adjacent ranks enough to route dense
   feedback circuits. Only if *that* also fails does the server fall back to
   labeled-net stubs (`buildFallbackCircuitDiagram`).
5. **Local ground rail.** The ground net anchors just below the lowest grounded
   pin, so ground drops are short and clustered under their parts instead of
   routing the full height of the sheet.
6. **Crop to content.** On success, `cropDiagramToContent` trims the canvas to a
   tight box around the drawn geometry (resize-only, never moves a coordinate, so
   a validated layout stays valid) so the schematic fills its frame.

`toDiagramSvg` (in `pcbGenerator.js`) and the canvas (`CircuitDiagram.jsx`)
render pin-number labels only on multi-pin parts that need them (op amps,
generic symbols); two-pin passives are unnumbered the way a schematic normally
is, and BJTs rely on their C/B/E letters. The canvas draws wires, symbols,
junction dots, and bridge arcs in a single ink (`--color-text`), reserving the
accent color for hover/selection; net-label pills are invisible until
hovered/selected so net names read as plain text flags. Layout tuning constants
(`TIDY_GRID`, `SPREAD_GRID`, clearances, `CANVAS_MARGIN`) live at the top of
`schematicLayout.js`.

#### Slot grid and slot-anchored placement

Generated components are placed into a **fixed slot grid** rather than scattered
freely. The grid is the source of truth: `SCHEMATIC_SLOT_LAYOUT` (in
`schematicLayout.js`) fixes the slot size (`slotWidth`/`slotHeight`), `gap`,
`margin`, and column count; `slotCanvasSize(rows, cols)` *derives* the canvas
from the grid (the inverse of the old canvas-drives-slots relationship), and
`slotGridFor(count)` grows the row count so every component gets its own slot
(≥2 rows). All slot geometry lives in **core** (`slotRects`, `slotCenter`,
`assignSlots`, `nextFreeSlot`, `nearestSlot`) so the layout engine and the
renderer share one definition.

`layoutCircuitDiagram(circuit, { slots: true })` is the slot-placement mode:
`buildDraft`'s `slots` branch sizes the canvas from the grid, orders components
by signal-flow rank, assigns them column-major to slot cells (`assignSlots`), and
pins each component **exactly** at its slot center (bypassing the
collision-nudge). The existing A\* router then wires them through the gaps, and
external-terminal **ports** drop as short stubs beside their connected pin
(edge-anchored ports would be unroutable across fixed slots). The returned
diagram carries `slotLayout: { rows, cols }`; `cropDiagramToContent` never shrinks
below the slot-grid extent, so empty trailing slots stay visible. `buildCircuitDiagram`
(hence the server's generated diagram and every edit through `synchronizeResult`)
and the canvas **Auto-layout** button all use `{ slots: true }`.

`geometry.js` `addedComponent` drops a manually-added part into the
`nextFreeSlot`; **dragging stays unrestricted** (`movedComponent` is unchanged) —
snapping a drag into a slot is a later step, as is mapping a component's terminals
onto the slot pins. `preserveDiagramLayout` keeps user-dragged positions and grows
the canvas to contain them, so a part dragged past the grid is not shoved back.

The canvas draws the slots as dashed, numbered rectangles below the wires and
symbols. `schematicSlots(rows, cols)` in `geometry.js` wraps core's `slotRects`
and decorates each with the **fixed pin set** (`SCHEMATIC_SLOT_PINS`: `+`/`−`
signal pins on the left, `V+`/`V−` power rails on top/bottom) — always present so
a component that occupies a slot maps its terminals onto the pins it uses. The
renderer sizes the grid from `diagram.slotLayout` (falling back to
`slotGridFor(components.length)`); the layer is `pointerEvents="none"`.

## Theming (light / dark)

Two palettes, both neo-brutalist: the original cream-paper light theme and a warm-charcoal
dark theme. The mechanism:

- **All colors are CSS custom properties** in `src/styles.css`. Beyond the surface/text
  variables, four semantic roles carry the theme flip: `--color-ink` (every border, wire,
  structural line — near-black in light, warm off-white in dark), `--color-chip` (raised
  button/card slabs — white in light, dark umber in dark), `--color-shadow` (the hard offset
  shadows — black in both, pure black in dark), and `--color-on-accent` (text sitting on the
  bright flat accents — dark in **both** themes, because the accent colors stay saturated in
  dark mode; only their `-soft` tints go deep). No stylesheet hardcodes `#111111`/`#ffffff`
  anymore — new CSS must use the variables.
- **`:root[data-theme='dark']`** in `styles.css` overrides the palette; `color-scheme` flips
  with it so native form controls/scrollbars follow.
- **`src/app/theme.js`** owns the rules: stored choice (`localStorage['pcb_theme']`) wins,
  else the OS `prefers-color-scheme`; `applyTheme` stamps `<html data-theme>` and syncs
  `<meta name="theme-color">`. `index.html` runs the same resolution inline **before first
  paint** so a saved dark theme never flashes light. Keep the two in sync if the rules change.
- **`src/app/ThemeToggle.jsx`** is the sun/moon button: rendered inside the workspace user
  bar, and as a fixed floating chip (`.theme-toggle-floating`) on pages without the bar
  (landing, waveform). `App.jsx` holds the `theme` state and persists on change.
- Physical hardware artwork (breadboard, parts, wire colors, serial-monitor terminal) keeps
  its real-world colors in both themes by design; only the bench mat (`--color-bench`)
  darkens.

## Persistence model

Everything user-facing is `localStorage`-backed (no server-side chat storage): the chat
store (`prompt-to-pcb-chats-v1`), the editor split layout
(`prompt-to-pcb-editor-split-v1`), and the theme choice (`pcb_theme`). Per-chat persisted geometry lives on the chat record:
`editedDiagram` (schematic edits) and `editedBreadboard` (breadboard part-placement anchors,
`{ version, parts: { [ref]: { strip, column } } }`) — both survive reloads and are reconciled
against the circuit when topology changes. Nothing lives server-side for the frontend anymore
(the dormant accounts backend still exists — see `docs/BACKEND.md`).
